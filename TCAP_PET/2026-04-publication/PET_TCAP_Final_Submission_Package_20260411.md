## 3. Executive Summary

New York City’s current housing-assistance architecture is effective at moving public dollars quickly, but weak at creating durable public or household assets. The controlling project draft argues that a portion of those recurring expenditures can be redirected, in carefully bounded circumstances, into tenant acquisition of distressed or at-risk multifamily buildings through limited-equity cooperative structures. The policy vehicle for that approach is the Public Equity Transfer (PET) framework and its programmatic arm, the Tenant Co-op Acquisition Program (TCAP).

This package recommends that the Mayor’s Office and City Council advance a **pilot-first**, **appropriation-dependent**, **legally narrowed** PET/TCAP framework rather than a citywide immediate mandate. The current official policy environment supports that posture. In 2026, the Administration re-established the Mayor’s Office to Protect Tenants through Executive Order 03, created the Rental Ripoff Hearings through Executive Order 08, launched Neighborhood Builders Fast Track and ELURP-linked acceleration for city-owned land, and HPD began implementing Local Law 86 rent-transparency notices. The FY26 state housing budget also created the first-year HAVP pilot and authorized a preservation tool for some large buildings with expiring affordability restrictions to partially convert to condominiums under ongoing regulatory oversight.

The controlling fiscal posture is narrower and more credible than prior drafts. Under v5.2, TCAP is modeled as costing approximately 3 percent more per household per year than CityFHEPS during the acquisition/stabilization period while generating roughly **$66,500** in long-run household equity under the project’s baseline assumptions. The controlling capital assumptions are **$128,000-$200,000** per unit with a **$165,000** midpoint for single-channel pilot budgeting, and approximately **$212,969** per unit under the borough-weighted three-channel model. The controlling break-even assumptions are **Years 12-13** for the single-channel baseline and **Years 23-27** for the weighted three-channel model. The controlling direct ROI framing is **30.8 percent direct asset creation**, with **6.2:1 societal ROI** treated separately as an externality-inclusive modeled estimate rather than a budget-book savings figure.

The package’s legal posture must remain disciplined. The City can lawfully build a program around cooperative carrying charges, pilot selection, tenant organizing support, a special fund subject to appropriation, voluntary-sale purchase opportunities, mixed-tenure safeguards, and evaluation of shared-equity models on public land. But the City cannot unilaterally rewrite federal voucher rules, compel use of federal funds in prohibited ways, override state cooperative/disclosure law, or promise categorical public-benefit resource exclusions for restricted equity. The project’s own challenge memos correctly identify that full implementation requires state enabling language, careful HUD positioning, and a stronger legal authorities appendix than currently exists.

### Decision requested

1. Approve PET as a pilot framework for EO 08 housing-policy implementation.
1. Authorize HPD, with HRA/DSS, NYCHA where applicable, MOPT, OMB, and Law, to prepare a **500-1,000 unit FY27 pilot**.
1. Advance four companion local laws in narrowed form.
1. Advance an Albany-side enabling package in parallel.
1. Direct agencies to prepare the missing submission-armor documents: legal authorities appendix, HUD/waiver memo, fiscal assumptions workbook, mixed-tenure implementation memo, interagency charter, and litigation-risk memo.

## 4. Final Policy Proposal

### 4.1 Purpose

The purpose of this proposal is to establish a legally bounded and fiscally reviewable pathway for New York City to redirect a lawful portion of recurring housing-assistance expenditures toward long-term affordability, tenant stability, and collective asset formation through limited-equity cooperative and community land trust structures. The package is intended for pilot implementation first, with expansion conditioned on legal, fiscal, and operational validation.

### 4.2 Problem Statement

The controlling project narrative describes NYC’s current housing-subsidy architecture as structurally extractive: the City pays recurring rent and shelter costs that generally do not create a public asset, a tenant asset, or a durable exit from subsidy dependence. The same files note operational strain in voucher issuance, lease-up, and subsidized unit abatement. The Preliminary FY2026 Mayor’s Management Report supports the operational side of that claim: HPD reported a median **137 days** from Section 8 voucher issuance to lease-up and **7 percent** of subsidized units in abatement in the preliminary FY2026 report.

The equity case is also supported by research, though it should be framed carefully. The project’s historical equity argument is strongest when it relies on the documented relationship between homeownership, wealth formation, and racialized housing inequality, rather than on abstract rhetoric. Elmelech’s study of New York City found large racial and ethnic disparities in homeownership and shelter-cost burden, including especially low homeownership rates and high rent burdens for several Latino and immigrant groups.

### 4.3 Why existing tools are insufficient

Existing city tools already address pieces of the problem but not the whole machine:

- MOPT now exists again as a tenant-protection coordinating office, but it is not itself an ownership-transfer mechanism.
- EO 08 created a hearing and interagency enforcement structure focused on abusive landlord practices, but hearings alone do not convert distressed buildings into tenant-controlled assets.
- Local Law 86 improves transparency for rent-stabilized tenants, but transparency without an acquisition pathway leaves tenants better informed and still structurally exposed.
- Neighborhood Builders Fast Track and ELURP can accelerate production on public land, but absent a shared-equity requirement they remain neutral as to whether residents build collective ownership or remain renters.

### 4.4 Current legal and administrative context

The current context supports a **two-track** PET/TCAP structure:

1. **Track One: Distressed-building acquisition / transfer**, focused on existing privately owned multifamily buildings with chronic distress and subsidy concentration.
1. **Track Two: Public-land shared-equity pilots**, focused on city-financed or city-controlled projects on public land, where legal risk is lower and design control is stronger.

The City’s administrative footing is stronger in 2026 than it was when earlier TCAP drafts were written. EO 03 requires broad agency cooperation with MOPT. EO 08 creates a hearing-and-policy platform for rental abuse. HPD has begun implementing LL 86. The Administration has also launched Neighborhood Builders Fast Track to reduce pre-development time on city-owned land by eight months, and paired it with ELURP implementation. Meanwhile, the FY26 state budget created a first-year HAVP pilot and authorized a partial condo preservation pathway for certain 100+ unit NYC rental buildings with expiring affordability restrictions.

### 4.5 PET framework overview

PET is the policy principle that housing dollars should create durable affordability and, where legally feasible, collective resident equity rather than only recurring rent outflows. TCAP is the program mechanism that operationalizes that principle through acquisition, rehabilitation, transfer, and governance. The current project files properly narrow the mechanism to cooperative carrying charges and pro-rata blanket-mortgage amortization rather than direct subsidy-funded share purchase. That legal narrowing is essential, not cosmetic.

### 4.6 TCAP program design

The recommended program design has four core features.

**First, building selection.** Eligible properties are screened based on subsidy concentration, physical distress, organizing readiness, title and lien complexity, and neighborhood stabilization value. Persistently distressed buildings can enter a Distressed Building PET Review.

**Second, tenant choice.** No lawful occupant is forced to buy in. Non-purchasing households remain protected occupants through department-approved leases or occupancy instruments. Mixed-tenure protections are mandatory.

**Third, financing.** The city package uses an appropriation-dependent Tenant Opportunity Fund to support acquisition financing, rehabilitation, legal and technical assistance, reserve seeding, and monitoring. Savings are not automatically swept; they may only be appropriated through the budget process.

**Fourth, equity accounting.** For federal vouchers, the lawful core is cooperative carrying charges. Under 24 CFR 982.619, the carrying charge may include a member’s share of blanket debt service, operating expenses, and reserve-fund payments, but may not include down-payments or amortization of a family purchase loan. That is why the controlling drafts now use pro-rata blanket mortgage amortization as the equity mechanism.

### 4.7 Eligibility and building selection

The final package should authorize HPD to adopt a scoring rubric that evaluates:

- share of subsidy-assisted households,
- repeated housing-maintenance violations and AEP history,
- harassment findings or comparable enforcement history,
- municipal liens and title complexity,
- tenant-organizing readiness,
- financial feasibility of acquisition and rehab,
- and neighborhood stabilization benefit.

This should be paired with a building-readiness worksheet and an interagency review protocol. The project’s internal path memo is correct that this operational documentation is still missing and needs to be produced before final submission-grade rollout.

### 4.8 Tenant choice and mixed-tenure protections

Mixed tenure is one of the most litigation-sensitive issues in the package. The current city bill draft handles this better than earlier versions by requiring a tenant transition plan covering non-purchasing households, interim lease and occupancy rights, arrears treatment, language access, disability accommodation, succession, and internal grievance procedures. Final submission should preserve those requirements and not downgrade them into narrative fluff.

### 4.9 Financing and fiscal architecture

The controlling fiscal narrative is as follows:

- CityFHEPS baseline: **$21,154 per household per year** (internal project figure)
- HCV baseline: **$16,896 per household per year** (internal project figure)
- TCAP stabilized annual cost: **~$21,800 per household per year** (internal modeled figure)
- Household equity: **~$66,500** under the project’s long-run baseline
- Per-unit capital: **$128,000-$200,000 midpoint $165,000** for the single-channel baseline; **~$212,969** for the weighted three-channel model
- Break-even: **Years 12-13** single-channel; **Years 23-27** three-channel
- Direct ROI: **30.8%**
- Societal ROI: **6.2:1** externality-inclusive modeled estimate.

Those numbers should be described honestly as internal modeled outputs from the controlling project draft, not as agency-certified fiscal facts. The project’s own feasibility memos are right that a CFO-proof workbook is still needed.

The external research case for acting on housing distress is still useful. Chernick, Reschovsky, and Newman found that the direct effect of the housing bust was responsible for about **21 percent** of the decline in general revenues for large central cities between 2009 and 2011, supporting the broader proposition that housing distress has material municipal fiscal effects.

### 4.10 Legal feasibility and required enabling actions

The package is **not** currently supported by a single complete legal pathway. The controlling challenge memo is clear: the City can build major parts of the framework, but full implementation still requires coordinated city legislation, state enabling language, and federal administrative positioning. The package is therefore best described as a **pilot-capable, scale-contingent** reform.

### 4.11 Interagency implementation plan

The final package should direct:

- **HPD** to lead program administration and rulemaking,
- **HRA/DSS** to handle city subsidy participation,
- **NYCHA / relevant PHA** to participate when federally administered vouchers are implicated,
- **MOPT** to serve as intake, complaint aggregation, and tenant-facing entry point,
- **OMB** to certify fiscal modeling and appropriation posture,
- **Law** to review preemption, benefit-eligibility, and enforcement-risk questions.

### 4.12 Data, reporting, and accountability

The City should require biennial fiscal reporting, annual fund reporting, annual project-level reporting, and public reporting on pilot evaluations and barriers to expansion. Because the City has already moved to a dynamic MMR platform, PET/TCAP reporting should be designed to fit the City’s broader accountability architecture rather than becoming an orphan spreadsheet.

### 4.13 Equity and civil-rights safeguards

The package should retain a fair-housing and anti-displacement frame without making legally sloppy promises. The strongest current footing is:

- race-neutral but equity-conscious screening criteria,
- language access,
- disability accommodation,
- anti-retaliation protections,
- preservation of existing non-waivable tenant protections absent voluntary election into approved alternative structures,
- and annual reporting on participation and outcomes.

### 4.14 Risks and mitigations

Major risks are:

- federal preemption / HUD refusal,
- state-law cooperative and disclosure barriers,
- mixed-tenure disputes,
- title, lien, and bankruptcy complexity in distressed buildings,
- underestimation of staffing and administrative burden,
- and litigation by owners, lenders, or opponents. The challenge memos identify all of these and should be treated as binding caution notes, not side chatter.

### 4.15 Pilot structure and timeline

The recommended structure is:

- FY27 pilot authorization for **500-1,000 units**,
- initial emphasis on legally clean acquisition opportunities and/or public-land shared-equity pilots,
- use of HAVP and any other cleaner external streams where lawfully available,
- evaluation before scale-up.

### 4.16 Decision points for Mayor and Council

The Mayor and Council should decide:

1. whether to adopt the pilot-first PET framework;
1. whether to sponsor Local Laws A-D in narrowed form;
1. whether to request Albany-side enabling legislation in parallel;
1. whether to fund the program through an appropriation-dependent capital line and fund structure in FY27;
1. whether to require the missing legal and fiscal appendices before broader public rollout.

## 5. Final City Legislative Package

### Package overview

The controlling city package should proceed as four coordinated local laws plus a package-wide savings clause and implementation note. The current cleaned bill-format package is strong enough to circulate for policy review but still requires conforming edits, numbering harmonization, and cross-reference cleanup before introduction.

### Local Law A - PET/TCAP Program Creation and Governance

Local Law A should:

- establish PET/TCAP in the Administrative Code,
- define carrying charge, certified tenant association, CLT, convertible subsidy stream, eligible property, persistently distressed building, and SLEC,
- assign HPD as lead administrator,
- assign HRA/DSS, NYCHA where applicable, MOPT, and OMB roles,
- require a transparent selection rubric,
- and preserve tenant choice and mixed-tenure protections.

### Local Law B - Tenant Opportunity Fund and Fiscal Architecture

Local Law B should:

- establish the Tenant Opportunity Fund as a lawful city fund structure,
- make expenditures subject to appropriation and ordinary budget/procurement law,
- define permitted uses,
- permit capitalization through appropriations, grants, recoveries, and other lawful sources,
- require biennial fiscal impact reporting,
- and expressly reject any automatic sweep or off-book entitlement theory.

### Local Law C - Tenant Purchase Opportunities, Distressed-Building Review, and Anti-Retaliation

Local Law C should:

- require notice of **voluntary sale**,
- create a right of first opportunity and right of first refusal on substantially equivalent bona fide sale terms,
- forbid interpretation that coerces owners to stay in federal/state programs or sell at administratively imposed prices,
- create Distressed Building PET Review,
- preserve due process for public designations,
- and include anti-retaliation and tenant-transition safeguards.

### Local Law D - Public-Land Shared-Equity Annex

Local Law D should:

- apply to new construction and substantial rehabilitation on city-financed or city-controlled land,
- require evaluation of shared-equity tenure structures where lawful and feasible,
- authorize equity-ladder pilots,
- require regulatory agreements,
- and require annual reporting on barriers to expansion.

### Package-wide savings clause

The package-wide savings clause should remain intact:

- no use of federal funds in prohibited ways,
- no override of state lien-priority, foreclosure, bankruptcy, corporate, or securities law absent separate amendment,
- no diminution of tenant protections absent knowing and voluntary election,
- no requirement that any agency act beyond lawful authority.

### Implementation note

The implementation note should expressly state that this legislative package is stronger than earlier repo versions but still requires:

- legal authorities appendix,
- state enabling bill text,
- HUD / waiver matrix,
- fiscal assumptions workbook,
- mixed-tenure implementation memo,
- interagency operating charter,
- and litigation risk memo.

## 6. Final State Enabling Companion

The Albany companion should be framed as a **model enabling bill** rather than final introduced text. The current draft correctly states why it is needed: current law does not cleanly recognize subsidy-backed limited-equity cooperatives as a distinct category, and the City should not pretend it can unilaterally solve state cooperative, public-assistance, or disclosure law.

The final state companion should therefore:

1. recognize SLECs as a regulated cooperative category,
1. authorize lawful capitalization / carrying-charge support where not prohibited by federal law,
1. establish restricted-equity treatment rules for state-supervised public-assistance purposes without promising blanket federal exemptions,
1. create affordability, resale, inheritance, and governance rules,
1. authorize a streamlined disclosure / offering-plan regime for tenant-initiated affordability conversions under government supervision,
1. and authorize local pilots.

The current companion draft already does this in concept. It should remain explicitly labeled as a **discussion draft for legislative counsel review**, with final section placement reserved for Albany counsel.

## 9. Known Limits / Open Questions

1. **HUD / federal uncertainty.** The City can build around cooperative carrying charges, but cannot claim authority to use federal voucher funds for direct share purchase or household-loan amortization absent additional authority.
1. **State-law gap.** SLECs are not yet a clean statutory category under New York law; the Albany companion remains necessary.
1. **Martin Act / offering-plan uncertainty.** The package still needs a disclosure / AG review memo.
1. **Mixed-tenure risk.** The bills are better than earlier drafts, but mixed-tenure implementation still requires a dedicated operational memo and template.
1. **Acquisition mechanics.** Distressed-building transfer still requires a real mechanics memo covering liens, judgments, 7A limits, foreclosure sequencing, bankruptcy risk, and due process.
1. **Administrative capacity.** The project still understates the staffing and interagency coordination burden unless the interagency charter is completed and funded.
1. **Political exposure.** The package is more defensible as a pilot than as an immediate citywide entitlement redesign.
1. **Current-policy claims requiring further verification.** Any claim about current CityFHEPS litigation posture, pending court outcomes, or newly proposed federal disparate-impact or qualified-immunity developments should be independently reverified before inclusion as a hard fact because those items were not relied on here as controlling official sources.
